Welcome to this visual guide on Gaussian Elimination.

In this video, we will learn how to solve a system of three linear equations using a systematic mathematical method.

Let's understand each step, perform the required row operations, and finally verify our answer.

Consider the following system of equations.

Our first equation is x plus y plus z equals six.

The second equation is two x plus y minus z equals one.

And the third equation is x minus y plus two z equals five.

Our objective is to find the values of x, y, and z that satisfy all three equations.

The first step is to convert these equations into an augmented matrix.

We write the coefficients of x, y, and z in the first three columns, and the constants in the last column.

The first non-zero entry in a row is called a pivot.

A pivot acts as the starting point for eliminating other entries in its column. By choosing pivots carefully, we gradually simplify the matrix.

We use pivots to eliminate the entries below them.

Our first pivot is one.

To eliminate the two in the second row, we subtract twice the first row from the second row.

Next, we subtract the first row from the third row.

After these operations, the first column contains zeros below the pivot.

Notice that we have not changed the solution of the original system. These elementary row operations produce an equivalent system with the same answers.

Now, we move to the second pivot, which is negative one.

We use the second row to eliminate the negative two in the third row.

After performing the operation, the third row becomes zero, zero, seven, and twenty-one.

Our next step is to divide every element in the third row by seven.

This gives us the final pivot, which is one, and the constant becomes three.

We have now reached row echelon form.

Notice the staircase pattern. Each leading entry moves to the right as we move down the rows, and all entries below each pivot are zero.

Now we solve the equations starting from the bottom row.

The third row gives us z equals three.

Substituting this value into the second row gives negative y minus nine equals negative eleven.

Adding nine to both sides gives negative y equals negative two.

Therefore, y equals two.

Now substitute y equals two and z equals three into the first equation.

We get x plus two plus three equals six.

Therefore, x equals one.

We have now calculated all three unknowns. The important idea of back substitution is to start with the easiest equation at the bottom and work upwards.

Let's understand the row operations through individual calculations.

For the second row, we subtract twice the first row, entry by entry.

Two minus two times one gives zero.

One minus two times one gives negative one.

Negative one minus two times one gives negative three.

And one minus two times six gives negative eleven.

Therefore, our new second row is zero, negative one, negative three, and negative eleven.

We follow the same process for the third row.

Then we use the updated second row to eliminate the second-column entry.

This gives us the final row: zero, zero, seven, and twenty-one.

Dividing by seven gives z equals three.

Let's verify our answer by substituting the values into the original equations.

One plus two plus three equals six.

Two times one plus two minus three equals one.

And one minus two plus two times three equals five.

All three equations are satisfied.

This verification is important because it confirms that our calculated values solve the original system, not just the transformed matrix.

Therefore, our final solution is x equals one, y equals two, and z equals three.

This completes our step-by-step explanation of Gaussian Elimination.
